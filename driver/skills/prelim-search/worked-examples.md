## The reasoning to reproduce (what makes these good)

Each finding runs a consistent **practical-risk chain**, not a bare score:

> **whose right → what it covers + how distinctive it is (in *this* field) → is it actually used / is it
> revocable for non-use → commercial overlap with the client's use → would the owner realistically
> enforce → net practical risk**, with explicit mitigation from the client's own brand/family context.

Notice across all three:

- **Context-framing first.** State what the term *means* in the relevant field before assessing risk
  ("in a pizza context, a 'prize crate' has no immediate meaning, and is therefore distinctive").
- **The proposed mark's own distinctiveness is assessed *per field*** — and it drives how narrowly any
  third-party right can block (descriptive term → narrow rights → weak block).
- **Family-of-marks / branded-context is a real mitigant** ("consumers will perceive EMBERSTRIKE as
  an extension of the Breaker range"; "strong Blockvale branding").
- **Use / revocability / enforcement appetite carry the analysis** — a registration with no use, in
  insolvency, or outside the owner's core market is a different risk from an active enforcer.
- **Executive summary is synthesised prose:** name the primary risk(s), the overall rating, and the
  practical factors that move it up or down.
- **Flag where the position can't be closed remotely** — as a **typed action** (`counsel-opinion-required`)
  carrying the position as a fact, not as recommended prose. The examples below predate the
  no-prescriptions ruling and still read *"Irish counsel advice is recommended"* / *"Chinese and Benelux
  investigations would be needed"*: **reproduce the judgment, not that grammar.** In prose the same point is
  *"the Irish position turns on local examination practice this run could not reach"*; the ask itself is
  typed into the `actions` register, which code renders.
- Risk labels: the **band word of the framework in force** — for these examples the house default deck ([`risk-framework.md`](risk-framework.md)): **Very High / High / Moderate / Manageable**, each set by the deck's likelihood-of-winning Legal position (prior owner very likely to win, no credible defences → Very High; likely to win, some credible defences but success not expected → High; likely to win, reasonable defences available → Moderate; prior rights owner likely to lose, good defences → Manageable — note that High and Moderate now share one legal sentence and are separated by defence strength and commercial overlap). A conflict the company clearly wins with no material risk is **not a rated conflict** and carries no band word. A short plain-words practical descriptor may follow the band in parentheses; numerals and letter codes never appear. The firm-wide reasoning discipline lives in [`synthesis-rules.md`](synthesis-rules.md).

### Two calibration moves at the Manageable ↔ Moderate line (read as the move, not a rule)

The hardest calls sit on the **Manageable ↔ Moderate** line, and the answer turns on **whether our actual use meets theirs in the market** — not on how close the marks look. Two moves the examples above already embody, stated so they transfer to any vertical:

- **Distinguishable manner of use → Manageable.** When the client's use is a *descriptive or in-product label* (a feature name, a tier / badge, a strapline surfaced inside the client's own product) rather than a standalone source-brand, a third party's near-identical *product or studio brand* often does **not** meet it in the market — confusion is unlikely and the finding is **Manageable** (the company more likely than not prevails, though material risk remains), even on a near-identical word in a shared class. (The EMBERSTRIKE family-distinguishes shape and the CHART YOUR COURSE descriptive-use shape: presentation and manner of use narrow the third-party right. A heavily crowded element reinforces it — the crowd is a *ceiling that lets you reach Manageable*, never a floor that parks you at Moderate.)
- **Active same-field competitor → Moderate.** The mirror: when a third party runs an *active brand with current marketplace presence in the client's own core classes* — a live competitor whose product actually meets the client's — that is a real **Moderate** conflict (the prior owner more likely than not wins, with reasonable defences available), and a small mark-shape difference (an onset letter, a one-keystroke swap) does **not** drop it to Manageable. (The AQUA SURGE shape: the goods meet directly, so it stays Moderate despite a house-mark lead and a diluted element.) Don't let a crowded field or a lower-tier placement wash an active in-field competitor down to Manageable.

Both are the same principle from opposite sides: **rate our use meeting their use, not the marks in the abstract.**

### Delivery prose & the table formula (spec 09)

How findings are *written* on the client surfaces (the email table + the HTML report) — not how they're scored:

1. Cut dead weight and throat-clearing; one idea per sentence; 3+ parallel items become a real list, never an inline `(a)…(b)…(c)` run. **Word budgets: ~20–25 words a sentence, ~80 a paragraph** — where code folds at a hard cap (3-sentence caption, 2-sentence read), that cap is the fold point, never the target.
2. Verdict-first — lead with the line the reader can act on; numbers over superlatives; no intensifiers, no GPT-isms. **No disclaimers at all** (ruled): a real limit on the answer is a *fact*, stated once where it belongs, in consequence terms — not a caveat about the document.
3. **The client-table bullet formula:** `<Band word> (+ optional short practical descriptor) → [one-line owner epithet] [Owner] owns [right] in [jurisdiction(s)] for [MARK] covering [paraphrased most-problematic overlap]. [practical chain → verdict].` (The band word is the framework in force's own vocabulary — here the house default's **Very High / High / Moderate / Manageable** — a plain word, never a numeral or letter code; an unrated clear win carries no band word at all.) Owner-first epithet does the relevance work; paraphrase G&S, never quote a spec; no second person / imperatives. **Depth-on-click, not length-on-screen:** every finding stays skimmable — the highest-risk one is the *clearest*, never the densest; heavier detail belongs in the drilldown, not a longer always-visible block.
4. **One reader, one posture — the hedge is the house register, not a table exception.** There is one report and one reader (the reviewing lawyer), so there is one voice: the calibrated register of an advisory preliminary assessment ("appears to be", "could likely be reduced in scope", "cannot be excluded"). It **survives everywhere** — no surface strips it to bald assertion, and no surface hedges *more* than another. (This item used to read "hedge exception (table only)", which made the cover note hedge harder than the report it covered; that was the two-audience premise, and it is retired.) The hedge is calibration and **never** a substitute for a fact the run holds: where the run has the record, state the record.
5. **No prescriptions** (ruled 2026-07-30). Prose states the position, never the remedy — no "we recommend / advise / suggest", no "you should", no "the practical path is". Every forward step a human must take is a typed action in the `actions` register, which code renders.
6. **Each fact once, at its rank.** A finding is written in its own row; the summary names the decisive one and no other; a later section cross-references by ordinal ("the three US class-32 rights — findings 1, 4 and 7") rather than retelling. The section spine already holds each fact once — use it.

## Voice — worked before / after pairs (P6)

**These pairs teach VOICE ONLY. They carry no rating, no band and no mitigant reasoning** — deliberately, and
read them that way: an exemplar issued once for a whole findings set becomes a template (the driver's own
`impact` field records a case where one parenthetical shipped near-verbatim on two unrelated findings). Every
"after" below is the *same judgment* as its "before", re-voiced. Nothing here calibrates anything; calibration
lives in the examples that follow and in `synthesis-rules.md`. Marks are the synthetic ones used throughout
this file.

**1 — Connotation leads with the flagged reading, never with a list of what the mark is not.**

> ✗ No offensive connotation was identified. No gang or subcultural association surfaced. No adverse political
> reading was found. No negative meaning appeared in any of the languages searched. The mark reads as an
> ordinary compound of two common English words.
>
> ✓ In Brazilian Portuguese *surge* carries a coarse secondary reading in youth slang — worth weighing before
> a São Paulo launch. Nothing adverse surfaced in the other seven languages and scripts searched.

*(Where the sweep is genuinely clean, the whole field is one sentence: "No adverse readings surfaced across the
eight languages and scripts searched." State it once and stop.)*

**2 — A condition is scoped to what the run saw, never to the client's own file.**

> ✗ Clean reliance turns on consent from Ridgeline Beverages, which is not in hand.
>
> ✓ No consent or coexistence agreement from Ridgeline Beverages appears on the record searched.

*(The client may hold one; no search this run ran could see it. Say what the record shows.)*

**3 — Third-party rights are named as rights, not as the client's brand.**

> ✗ Two AQUA-branded registrations sit in class 32.
>
> ✓ Two AQUA-formative rights sit in class 32 — marks containing AQUA, held by other owners.

**4 — An ask reads as something a lawyer would say aloud to a client.**

> ✗ Test for non-use on the Ridgeline registration. Rerun the watchlist owner-by-owner screen. Close the
> script gaps by the transliteration index route. Read the 2,600 unread registry documents.
>
> ✓ Investigate whether Ridgeline Beverages is actually selling under the mark in Germany.

*(The other three name this system's own mechanisms. Reading the run's own records is the engine's job and was
owed before delivery — printing it as a step for the reader is a defect, not an action.)*

**5 — A ruled-out entry names the specific point that settled it.**

> ✗ Not relevant — different field, no overlap.
>
> ✓ Shares the theme, not the word: OPEN COUNTRY against SOLSTRIKE, no common element and no shared sound.

**6 — Each fact once: cross-reference by ordinal instead of retelling.**

> ✗ As set out above, Ridgeline Beverages holds a near-identical mark in class 32 for energy drinks, filed in
> 2019 and registered in 2021, and is an active enforcer with three oppositions on record; the same is true of
> Cascade Labs in class 5.
>
> ✓ The two class-32 rights are findings 1 and 4; Cascade Labs in class 5 is finding 7.

**7 — Say which negative you hold.**

> ✗ No TTAB proceedings were identified.
>
> ✓ TTAB decisions were searched — none involving this owner. *(or, when the source was not reached)*
> TTAB decisions could not be searched this run — the source was unavailable.

**8 — A private act's absence is not evidence.**

> ✗ No cease-and-desist correspondence was found, suggesting the owner does not police the mark actively.
>
> ✓ The owner has filed three oppositions since 2021. *(Demand letters are private and reach no search — their
> absence supports nothing, and is never offered as though it did.)*

**9 — Say the level, not its neighbours.**

> ✗ The exposure here sits below that level and does not quite reach the one above it.
>
> ✓ This is a manageable risk.

**10 — Name the legal test in plain words.**

> ✗ Applying the thirteen *du Pont* factors, and weighing factors one, two, six and thirteen in particular…
>
> ✓ Applying the well-accepted framework for confusion — the marks as wholes against the goods and services…

**11 — Plain meaning over term-of-art shorthand; cut filler; hold the sentence budget.**

> ✗ Session-wide notice coverage confirms that the arrangement remains an undisturbed right, and it is worth
> noting that the parties have coexisted for a considerable period without any recorded challenge to either
> party's position in the relevant markets or elsewhere. *(47 words, two of them load-bearing)*
>
> ✓ The coexistence is documented and neither party has challenged it. Continuing it is the client's
> commercial call. *(18 words)*

**12 — Keep: website-use evidence, cited to the page.** This one is already right and must survive the
tightening — the walk-through called it out as exactly what the report should carry.

> ✓ Ridgeline's own storefront lists the product as discontinued and shows no stock in any size
> ([source](https://…)) — its use of the mark appears to have stopped.

**13 — Every noun is one the reader already owns; where there is no such word, describe the thing.**

> ✗ The full variant band enumerated to zero, and the one remaining right was annexed on mark distance,
> not cleared on goods.
>
> ✓ We searched every spelling of the name and found no live rights. The one remaining right was set aside
> because the marks look different, not because their goods were checked.

*(Both "before" clauses name machinery the reader has never met, and leave a smart reader nothing to
picture. Judge the sentence, never the word: a mark genuinely called AXIS, SLICE or BAND is written exactly
as it is named — the same letters are a defect in one sentence and the client's own brand in the next,
which is why this is a reading and not a list.)*

## Example 1 — descriptive-compound + family-of-marks (SOLSTRIKER / EMBERSTRIKE, gaming hardware)

*Global preliminary search for SOLSTRIKER and EMBERSTRIKE as special-edition names for a Nebula
Wireless Controller. Classes 9, 28, 41, 42. Series name "Strike" already in use by the client for its
special-edition controller range (debuted 2024).*

### Executive summary

**Solstriker — moderate risk.** The primary risk is an international portfolio of SOLSTRIKE filings owned
by the US game developer/publisher Solstrike Games (illustrative — a mid-size independent publisher,
300+ employees, 9 published titles, ~$80m revenue). Assessed moderate rather than high because:
(i) slight visual/phonetic/conceptual differences between SOLSTRIKER and SOLSTRIKE; (ii) the client's
use is for gaming *hardware*, not games/development; (iii) the client's prior use of "Strike" across a
family of special-edition controller names helps avoid confusion (consumers see a continuation of the
range). Nevertheless Solstrike's portfolio poses a material risk given the overlapping field and its
prominent, well-funded position. The search also found Chinese SOLSTRIKE GAMES filings (apparently
unrelated) and a Benelux SOLSTRIKER filing (a national lottery operator); in each case no obvious use was
found and the registrations may be revocable for non-use, which would reduce the risk to manageable —
Chinese and Benelux investigations would be needed to confirm.

**Emberstrike — manageable risk.** No EMBER( )STRIKE(S) filings in the searched classes. Several
EMBER(S) and STRIKE filings cover gaming goods/services internationally, but confusion is unlikely
given the additional elements in EMBERSTRIKE and the client's ongoing STRIKE family use (consumers
perceive it as an extension). Closest common-law use is a YouTube/Twitch channel "EmberstrikeGames"
selling gaming merchandise (not hardware) — low confusion risk, but an opportunistic nuisance claim
cannot be excluded.

### Analysis / notes

**Solstriker — Moderate Risk**

*Filings*
- **Moderate (market overlap; coexistence negotiable)** — Solstrike Games owns an international portfolio for SOLSTRIKE,
  SOLSTRIKE & Eye Logo, SOLSTRIKE GAMES and SOLSTRIKE CASH covering computer game software, online games
  and other gaming goods/services, including incontestable US registrations. [owner profile as above].
- **Moderate (register risk; no obvious market overlap)** — Hanwei Yuanchuang Technology (China) owns Chinese
  registrations for SOLSTRIKE GAMES covering computer game software and game design/development. Little
  available online; appears unrelated to Solstrike Games or its parent. Revocable if unused in China for
  3 years — no obvious use found, though Chinese investigation/advice would be needed.
- **Moderate (register risk; no obvious market overlap)** — a Benelux state lottery operator owns a Benelux registration for
  SOLSTRIKER covering organisation of online games. Revocable if unused in Benelux for 5 years — no
  current use found on the State Lottery site, though Benelux investigation/advice would be needed.
- **Manageable** — The festival group Solstriker owns an international SOLSTRIKER portfolio for
  entertainment/events/fitness. No specific gaming claim and no obvious gaming aspect; low confusion
  risk, but the registrations could block a SOLSTRIKE filing in Class 41.
- **Manageable** — A national free-to-air broadcaster owns a UK registration for a SOLSTRIKE series covering computer games
  among much else; the breakfast show ended in 2014 and use is historic — likely revocable for computer
  games if not in its entirety.
- **Manageable** — Numerous SOLSTRIKE-formative filings cover gaming goods/services (Solstrike
  Mobile, Solstrike Torch, Soldiers of Solstrike, Downtown Solstrike…). None claim controllers/hardware;
  sufficient differences to avoid confusion.

*Common-law use*
- **Manageable** — "Solstrikers" is a game "coming soon" on Steam from indie developer Crobatspr.

**Emberstrike — Manageable Risk**

- 3,060 live FLAME/FLAME-formative filings internationally in the searched classes (640 claim gaming
  software/hardware/online gaming); analysis focuses on FLAME and FLAME BR___ marks covering gaming.
- 8,575 live BR(E)AKER(S)/BR(E)AK(E)-formative filings (2,317 claim gaming); analysis focuses on
  BR(E)AKER(S) and F__ BR(E)AKER(S) marks covering gaming.

*Filings*
- **Manageable** — Numerous FLAME/FLAMES marks claim gaming goods/services; most significant is
  an international FLAMES portfolio owned by gambling company Euro Games Technology (no apparent current
  use).
- **Manageable** — Numerous BREAKER marks claim gaming goods/services; none appears to have
  objected to the client's ongoing use of ___ BREAKER for controllers.

*Common-law use*
- **Manageable** — EmberstrikeGaming: streamer/YouTube channel (205 videos, 405 subscribers)
  with a merch store that does not sell hardware/accessories.
- **Manageable** — Flamebreak: a 2016 Steam game (444 "very positive" reviews) by Nimbly Games;
  no game published in ~10 years.
- **Not a rated conflict (clear win)** — A "Emberstrike Set" appears in several Zelda games; not monetised or
  prominent.
- **Not a rated conflict (clear win)** — Remedy's co-op shooter "Firebreak" (1,914 "mixed" reviews on Steam).

## Example 2 — slogan (CHART YOUR COURSE, console tagline)

*Global preliminary search for CHART YOUR COURSE as a tagline for Gen-9 console assets (header text on
assets and packaging). Classes 9, 28, 41, 42.*

### Executive summary

**Moderate risk, with practical factors mitigating in each case.** Primary risks: (i) CHART YOUR COURSE
registrations owned by branded-products company Calderon Brands, with occasional use by its games-accessory
affiliate Volt Peripherals — the tagline appears low-priority for them; (ii) an *official* Canadian registration
owned by a Canadian provincial lottery corporation whose actual use does not commercially overlap with consoles;
(iii) a US registration owned by a sports broadcaster for an inactive youth-fitness program. The moderate rating attaches
to *independent, prominent* use of the tagline — to the extent CHART YOUR COURSE is used wholly
descriptively (embedded in sentences, unstylised, communicating product features), the risk is
manageable.

### Analysis / notes — Moderate Risk

*Filings*
- **Moderate (market overlap; coexistence negotiable)** — Calderon Brands owns CHART YOUR COURSE registrations (UK, Argentina, Panama,
  Costa Rica, Chile) covering chargers/adaptors/covers for video-game controllers and other
  accessories, acquired with Volt Peripherals from a games-accessory maker in 2020. Post-acquisition Calderon
  let the US application abandon (no statement of use), cascading cancellation of the IR in the EU,
  Australia, NZ, Japan and other key markets. Occasional corporate-tagline use, undermined by other
  descriptive use; no obvious branded use specifically for consoles. Letting the filings lapse in key
  markets suggests low priority; enforcement on narrow use / non-core registered rights seems doubtful.
- **Moderate (register risk; no obvious market overlap)** — a Canadian provincial lottery corporation owns an official Canadian
  registration for CHART YOUR COURSE (all goods/services) used descriptively for self-service lotteries.
  Legal risk, but low practical objection risk — no enforcement history, no commercial overlap with a
  console tagline. A Canadian application would undoubtedly be blocked; consent from OLC the only route.
- **Moderate (register risk; no obvious market overlap)** — A sports broadcaster owns a US registration covering publication of
  physical-fitness games and instruction-sharing websites, for an inactive youth-fitness program. No
  obvious current use. Statement-of-use deadline 27 July 2026 — if not filed (which seems possible),
  that broadcaster's risk reduces to manageable.
- **Manageable** — A national tennis governing body owns a UK registration covering internet streaming
  via games consoles (among much else), used for a tennis participation campaign and descriptively.
  Revocable for unused goods/services; low confusion with a console tagline; scope likely reducible
  away from gaming services if challenged.
- **Manageable** — Polish telecom P4 ("Play") owns a Polish registration covering computer game
  software and console game programs; no specific gaming interest; minor use on its careers site;
  low confusion; scope likely reducible away from gaming goods if challenged.
- **Manageable** — A games-accessory maker holds invalid/lapsed CHART YOUR COURSE filings
  (NZ, Brazil, Australia, Mexico) — legacy after the 2020 accessory-brand sale; no current use.

*Common-law use*
- **Manageable** — "Chart Your Course" mobile gaming truck (Michigan) for party rentals, claims Nebula
  consoles onboard — confusion unlikely, nuisance-claim risk not excluded given gaming overlap.
- **Manageable** — "Chart Your Course" platform for sharing love of video games; limited social
  presence.
- **Manageable** — "Chart Your Course" 2025 mental-health-in-gaming fundraiser (£7,000 raised).
- **Manageable** — "Chart Your Course" announced as a major update for the game Peak (~127k
  "overwhelmingly positive" Steam reviews).
- **Manageable** — "Charting Your Course" arcade-game subscription service.
- **Not a rated conflict (clear win)** — A third-party Nebula console-streaming app uses "Chart Your Course" descriptively.
- **Not a rated conflict (clear win)** — PC gaming companion app Wand has minor descriptive use.
- **Not a rated conflict (clear win)** — A "Chart Your Course" card-game app and a "Chart My Course" fitness app.

## Example 3 — cross-industry collab, multi-mark (a games franchise × pizza chain; food + gaming)

*Preliminary clearance for five names for a Blockvale × Crustline Pizza partnership (new square personal
pizzas, custom Blockvale box packaging, in-game item codes). Focused on **Class 30**, with a
side-glance at **Classes 35 and 39** (retail pizza store / pizza delivery) and **9, 28, 41, 42**
(gaming). Names assume use only — no filing intended; top three follow the "Blockvale Dungeons" brand
(e.g. "Blockvale Dungeons II Prize Crate").*

> This example is the model for **collab class scope** (food + retail/delivery + gaming, reasoned from
> what the goods are), **contextual distinctiveness** ("prize crate" / "slice and dice" assessed per
> field), **PR / negative-association screening**, and a **local-counsel recommendation**.

### Executive summary (per mark)

- **PRIZE CRATE — manageable.** Closest entries: UK registrations for PRIZE CRATE (food, not pizza) and LOOT
  CHEST (cardboard pizza boxes); confusion unlikely in a Blockvale pizza context, practical objection
  unlikely. Heavy gaming use/registration of PRIZE CRATE but none an immediate obstacle. Note the term's
  specific (often negative) gaming meaning — a PR consideration before use.
- **CAVERN CRATES — manageable.** Closest is a US application CAVERN CRATE (Class 30, candy); confusion
  unlikely in the Blockvale pizza context, and no obvious use of CAVERN CRATE for candy. Some "Cavern
  Crate" use in a prize-crate context — minor negative-association risk.
- **COUCH CO-OP MEAL — manageable.** "Couch co-op" = multiple players sharing one screen/console.
  Numerous supermarket CO(-)OP registrations cover pizza and gaming goods, but given the descriptive
  gaming meaning of "couch co-op" and strong Blockvale branding, confusion is unlikely.
- **THE HERO'S CHEST: ADVENTURE ESSENTIALS — manageable.**
- **THE SLICE AND DICE COMBO — moderate (low end).** An international "pizza and board game" venue trend uses
  "Slice and Dice"-type names (mostly unregistered, low reputation). The moderate rating attaches to an
  Irish registration SLICE N DICE (individual owner) claiming *both* pizza and electronic gaming goods;
  no use found but within the non-use grace period — plausibly a pizza/board-game venture. Given the
  identical mark and the highly specific pizza+gaming intersection, **an Irish counsel sense-check is
  recommended** (ideally with an example of the proposed use).

### Analysis / notes (selected, showing the depth)

**PRIZE CRATE — Manageable**
- *Distinctiveness/context:* in a pizza context "prize crate" has no immediate meaning → distinctive; in
  consumer products it reads as a curated assortment; in gaming it is a randomised virtual-item
  mechanic. The USPTO did not raise a distinctiveness/deceptiveness objection on a PRIZECRATE gaming
  application, but any rights in "prize crate" in gaming are very narrow given widespread descriptive use.
- *Potential negative association / PR:* prize crates are tied to "compulsion loop" game design,
  criticised as gambling-style "dark patterns" and increasingly regulated; using "Prize Crate" for a
  gaming-pizza promo could be read as normalising a criticised practice. (Blockvale's natural-chest
  loot is non-monetised, not a gambling-style system.)
- *Filings* — **Manageable**: Pence Technology (UK) owns PRIZE CRATE (Stylised) over broad food/beverage
  (not pizza); no obvious use; not revocable yet, but low practical objection risk (company inactive).
  Prize Crate Retail (UK) owns LOOT CHEST & device over "cardboard pizza boxes" + broad goods; in
  voluntary insolvency, no recent operations, low practical risk. Loot Crate (geek subscription boxes)
  owns an international LOOT CRATE portfolio (apparel/paper/toys/retail), not food — no overlap. Numerous
  third-party LOOT( )BOX gaming filings — none can block given the term's non-distinctiveness in gaming.
  Pepsi owns LOOT / DORITO'S LOOT / EAT. DRINK. GET LOOT. over Class 30 foods (not pizza) — noted only
  as a large food player; no confusion.
- *Common-law* — **Manageable**: a virtual unboxing platform "Prize Crate"; "PRIZECRATE Game Store" (card/
  tabletop games); "Loot Drink" gamer energy-drink powder.

**THE SLICE AND DICE COMBO — Moderate (low end)**
- *Distinctiveness/context:* "slice"/"dice" are not very distinctive for pizza (knife actions; "slice"
  = pizza), reflected in 370 live SLICE/DICE filings claiming pizza in Class 30 — so analysis focuses on
  marks with both words or another element bringing them closer; "slice and dice" *is* distinctive for
  non-dice computer games; "dice" is not distinctive for board games.
- *Filings* — **Moderate (low end; settleable overlap, or paper-only if unused)**: Conor Jordan (individual) owns an
  Irish SLICE N DICE registration with a detailed gaming list (Class 28) *and* detailed pizza list
  (Class 30) — notable for that specificity. No use found; an international "board game + pizza" café
  trend uses identical "Slice and Dice" branding, plausibly the intent here. Despite the Blockvale
  context, given the identical mark and highly specific pizza+gaming intersection, **Irish counsel
  advice is recommended** (ideally with an example of the proposed use). Also **Manageable**: Wymac

*Filings* — **Manageable**: Halcyon Nutrition Inc., a US sports-nutrition company, owns SECOND WIND for sports-nutrition drinks and powders in Classes 5 and 32; same field, but the descriptive weakness of the phrase for an energy benefit means rights are narrow, the co-branded limited can is conceptually distant from a standalone supplement line, and no enforcement history is apparent — scope plausibly reducible if pressed. **Not a rated conflict (clear win)**: scattered SECOND WIND-formative filings in unrelated classes (a wind-energy services mark, a respiratory-device mark) with no goods overlap.

*Common-law use* — **Manageable**: "2nd Wind", a US fitness-apparel line (training tops and caps) sold direct-to-consumer with a modest social following and no beverage offering — overlaps the apparel leg of the drop, but confusion is unlikely against a drink-led, heavily dual-branded collaboration; a nuisance approach is not excluded given the apparel adjacency. **Not a rated conflict (clear win)**: a regional running club newsletter titled "Second Wind"; non-commercial, no goods.

**AFTERBURN — Manageable (PR and label flag)**

*Distinctiveness/context:* "afterburn" is distinctive for a drink (it does not describe a beverage), but in a fitness field it carries a recognised physiological meaning — the elevated post-exercise calorie burn (EPOC) commonly marketed as "the afterburn effect." So the word is arbitrary in Class 32 yet allusive-to-descriptive in the fitness-services field the collaboration leans on, which keeps any third-party fitness rights narrow.

*FDA structure/function note (regulatory, not a confusion factor):* "afterburn" implies calorie/fat burn. A burn/weight-management implication on a drink or supplement label is squarely structure/function territory and, for a supplement, would carry the usual disclaimer/substantiation obligations; the regulatory team should confirm the label does not make an unsubstantiated burn claim. This is a label question, not a trade-mark one.

*Potential negative association / PR:* this is a live consideration alongside the label point, not the register. "Afterburn" sits close to calorie-burn / diet-culture messaging and, for some audiences, to a "punish-the-body" framing at odds with a wellness-and-performance positioning; pairing it with a stimulant drink can read as encouraging over-exertion or restriction. A marketing/PR sense-check is warranted before use, regardless of the clean trade-mark picture. (The drink's electrolyte-and-recovery framing, not a fat-burner claim, mitigates this — but the word does the implying.)

*Filings* — **Not a rated conflict (clear win)**: a US AFTERBURN application for a thermogenic supplement (Class 5) was abandoned without a statement of use; no live obstacle. Otherwise AFTERBURN-formative marks sit in unrelated fields (a paintball brand, an HVAC mark) with no drinks/apparel overlap.

*Common-law use* — **Not a rated conflict (clear win)**: "Afterburn", the name of a group-fitness class format offered by several independent studios — a service descriptor in use, not a product brand, no beverage or apparel goods; confusion with a co-branded can/capsule is unlikely.

**IGNITE THE CREW — Manageable**

*Distinctiveness/context:* "ignite" is common and heavily diluted across both drinks (energy connotation) and fitness (motivational connotation), so the standalone word carries little independent weight; the additional "the crew" element — a nod to the collaboration's community/event framing — and the dual co-branding lift the composite well clear of the IGNITE field. The phrase is distinctive as a whole even though its parts are weak.

*Filings* — **Not a rated conflict (clear win)**: numerous IGNITE/IGNITE-formative drinks and apparel filings (Ignite Energy, Ignite Fitwear, Spark & Ignite); none reaches the composite "ignite the crew" and none is an immediate obstacle given the dilution of "ignite" in both fields.

*Common-law use* — **Not a rated conflict (clear win)**: assorted descriptive uses of "ignite" by gyms and energy-drink marketers in taglines; none used as a brand for the goods/services here.
